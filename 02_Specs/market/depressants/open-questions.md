# Open questions and contested claims

**Area:** [[depressants/index]]

## Contested or unresolved empirical questions

1. **Are nitazenes actually declining, or just being tested for less?**
   CFSRE reports a late-2025 nitazene decline with orphine replacement. But the same literature says nitazene detection is **limited by lab capacity and reference standards**, and a 2025 UK study argues nitazene deaths are **undercounted** because post-mortem concentrations fall below detection thresholds ([Clin Toxicol](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/15563650.2025.2601141)). A "decline" measured by detections in a system with shifting analytical scope is weak evidence. **Unresolved.**

2. **Did China's mid-2025 generic nitazene scheduling cause the orphine shift?**
   Widely asserted by CFSRE and in press coverage, and mechanistically plausible (class-wide control pushes substitution to a structurally distinct scaffold). But it is a **temporal inference**, not a demonstrated causal finding, and no counterfactual exists. **Contested framing, treat as hypothesis.**

3. **Has national xylazine prevalence peaked?**
   Philadelphia shows near-complete substitution (98% → 31%). National CDC data lags to 2023/Q1 2024 and still shows **growth**. These are compatible but do not answer the question. Compounded by xylazine having **no dedicated ICD-10 code** ([PMC13166611](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13166611/)). **Unresolved; probably unanswerable until 2027 data.**

4. **Does medetomidine cause necrotic wounds like xylazine?**
   No published answer found. Xylazine's soft-tissue wound burden is its signature harm; if medetomidine lacks it, the substitution is a partial improvement, and if it shares it, the wound epidemic will continue under a new name. **Major gap.**

5. **Dexmedetomidine vs racemic medetomidine in the illicit supply.**
   No surveillance data found distinguishing the two. Since dexmedetomidine is the active enantiomer, the distinction matters for potency per unit mass and for interpreting case reports. **Unknown.**

6. **What is BTMPS doing to people?**
   The JAMA authors list human health effects as an explicit open question. Most BTMPS-positive samples contain **more BTMPS than fentanyl by mass**, which makes "it's a trace contaminant" untenable, yet there is **no clinical toxicity literature**. Also unresolved: whether it is a synthesis artefact, a bulking agent, or a stabiliser. **The single largest unknown-exposure question in this area.**

7. **Why do BTMPS-positive samples have *lower* fentanyl content?** (3.1% vs 8.7%). Dilution, a different production line, or a correlated supply route — not established.

8. **Is 7-OH a genuine prodrug pathway for mitragynine's effects?**
   Mouse data suggest brain 7-OH levels could account for most of mitragynine's opioid analgesia, but the conversion fraction is low and the claim is **disputed**. This is not academic: if leaf-derived mitragynine substantially converts to 7-OH in humans, the regulatory line between leaf and concentrate is blurrier than either side wants.

9. **Does naloxone reliably reverse the orphines?**
   Expected on mechanistic grounds (μ-opioid agonists), and at least one non-fatal cychlorphine overdose has been described. But there is **no published clinical series** on antagonist response, dose requirements or duration for this class. Given SR-17018-type compounds in the same structural family have unusual signalling properties, assuming fentanyl-like reversal is **not yet evidence-based**.

10. **Is there a real "~10× fentanyl" figure for cychlorphine?**
    Circulating in press and treatment-centre material; **no primary source found**. UNODC says only "potency exceeding fentanyl". The nearest hard number is for the related **chlorphine** (Ki 1.92 nM vs fentanyl 6.29 nM) — a binding affinity, not a potency ratio. **Treat the 10× figure as unverified.**

11. **Are ethylbromazolam and clonazolam actually displacing bromazolam, or adding to it?**
    Scotland's RADAR data support clonazolam ascendancy there; the US picture is bromazolam-dominant with ethylbromazolam emerging. No market-share data exists for either, and DEA itself says bromazolam is under-reported. **Unresolved.**

12. **Gabapentinoid-involved deaths, 2025–26.**
    The most recent US death-level data located runs to **2019–2020**. Recent work is risk-association only. There is no current national count. **Surveillance gap, not a null finding.**

13. **Tianeptine poison-centre trend.**
    The 4 (2013) → ~350 (2024) trajectory comes from secondary reporting of FDA data; the primary tabulation was not located. **Unverified.**

14. **7-OH poison-centre figures.**
    901 cases in H1 2026 vs 593 in all of 2025 — cited by treatment-centre sites to America's Poison Centers, **not verified against the primary source**. **Unverified.**

15. **Is there genuinely nothing new in the GHB/GABA-B space?**
    My searches found no new GHB-like NPS class notified in 2025–26, only older medicinal-chemistry literature. That is a **negative result from a limited search**, not a confirmed absence. EUDA's "Other drugs" chapter and UNODC EWA should be checked directly.

16. ~~**Legal status of etizolam and flualprazolam in 2026.**~~ **US status CLOSED 2026-11-09:** both are **permanently US Schedule I, effective 1 April 2026** ([FR 2026-04112](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/03/02/2026-04112/schedules-of-controlled-substances-placement-of-clonazolam-diclazepam-etizolam-flualprazolam-and), Docket DEA-989), in the same final rule as flubromazolam, clonazolam and diclazepam, which also implements the 1971 Convention.
    **Still open outside the US:** jurisdiction-by-jurisdiction status is not resolved, and is genuinely complicated by etizolam being a **licensed medicine** in several countries (Japan, India, Italy) — a US Schedule I listing says nothing about its availability as a prescription product there.

17. ~~**Flubromazolam permanent US Schedule I from 1 April 2026**~~ — **CLOSED 2026-11-09. Verified:** final rule [FR 2026-04112](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/03/02/2026-04112/schedules-of-controlled-substances-placement-of-clonazolam-diclazepam-etizolam-flualprazolam-and), published 2 Mar 2026, effective 1 Apr 2026. The previously cited secondary source was right.

18. **Will the 7-OH threshold hold?**
    DEA's July 2026 notice was withdrawn and replaced in October 2026 after HHS revised the recommended threshold. **Confirmed against the Federal Register on 2026-11-09: no temporary order for 7-OH itself is in effect** — both 14 Oct 2026 documents are notices of intent, and no DEA action on 7-OH published between 14 Oct and 9 Nov 2026. What the new (higher) threshold actually is, whether a per-article or per-weight test survives comment and litigation, and whether it incidentally captures leaf products, remain open. DEA states expressly that **no finding of safety below the threshold has been made by either HHS or DEA**.

19. **Does medetomidine's spread westward continue?**
    CDC describes expansion Northeast → Midwest → South with only low-level Western detection, and LA County reports the severe withdrawal phenotype **not yet observed locally**. Whether the West is lagging or structurally different (different supply chains) is unknown.

20. **How much of the apparent nitazene/orphine/medetomidine "surge" is better testing?**
    NFLIS medetomidine reports tripled 2024→2025 while remaining <1% of all reports. Some of that is real growth; some is labs starting to look. **Nobody has cleanly separated the two for any substance in this area.**

## Methodological notes on this area's evidence base
- **Philadelphia and Pittsburgh dominate the medetomidine clinical literature.** The ICU and intubation rates are from overlapping cohorts in the highest-prevalence city in the country and should not be read as national expectations.
- **Surveillance counts are testing-capacity measurements.** For every substance in this area, prevalence and mortality figures are lower bounds whose tightness depends on lab scope, reference-standard availability and whether the substance has an ICD-10 code.
- **Drug-checking services are leading indicators.** Ethylbromazolam (Toronto, Nov 2024) and 5-cyano isotodesnitazene were detected by harm-reduction services well before forensic or regulatory systems. Any monitoring picture built only from seizures and death certificates is structurally late. See [[drug-checking]].
