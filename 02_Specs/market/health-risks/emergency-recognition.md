# Recognising acute emergencies

Up: [[health-risks/index]]

> **Scope and limits — read first.**
> This note is at the level of **published first-response and public-health guidance**: how to recognise an emergency and the fact that the response is to call emergency services. It contains **no self-treatment protocols, no drug doses, no "how to manage this at home"**, and nothing that substitutes for emergency care.
>
> Three things are true of every situation below:
> 1. **Call emergency services.** Every syndrome here can kill, several have no treatment outside hospital, and several look mild early.
> 2. **Stay with the person.** Most of these deteriorate over minutes to hours. Published harm-reduction guidance is consistent that using alone is the single biggest modifiable risk factor.
> 3. **Tell the responders what was taken, honestly and specifically, including that it was an unregulated product of uncertain identity.** Clinicians cannot test for most of these compounds. The medetomidine clusters below were missed precisely because nobody knew what to look for. In many jurisdictions Good Samaritan provisions protect people who call.

Related: [[polydrug-risk]] · [[drug-checking]]

## 1. Opioid overdose

**Recognise:** unresponsive or barely rousable · breathing slow, shallow, irregular or stopped · snoring/gurgling sounds (an obstructed airway, not sleep) · blue or grey lips, fingertips and face · pinpoint pupils · limp body.

**Published first-response guidance:**
- **Call emergency services.**
- **Give naloxone.** Give it even when an opioid is not certain, and even when an alpha-2 sedative or benzodiazepine is suspected, because opioids are almost always also present — in the Chicago medetomidine cluster **fentanyl was found in every drug sample and blood specimen containing medetomidine** ([MMWR 74(15):258–265](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/74/wr/mm7415a1.htm)).
- **More than one dose is often needed.** Public guidance is to repeat naloxone if breathing has not improved, following the interval in the local protocol (Washington State's protocol says a further dose every three minutes while continuing rescue breathing; the District of Columbia guide uses roughly three minutes of rescue breaths without spontaneous breathing before a repeat — **protocols differ, follow your local guidance**). Clinical data support repeat dosing for the more potent novel opioids: in a 537-patient emergency-department cohort, patients exposed to novel potent opioids received a significantly higher mean number of in-hospital naloxone boluses (**1.33 vs 0.36** for fentanyl), and metonitazene cases averaged **3.0** doses — though that subgroup was only 2 patients, and 2 of 2 received CPR ([Amaducci et al., JAMA Netw Open 2023, PMID 37642962](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37642962/) · [PMC10466160](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10466160)). Australian guidance states higher and/or repeated doses may be required given nitazene potency ([NCCRED briefing](https://nccred.org.au/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/Nitazenes-Emerging-Drug-Briefing.pdf)). Note the evidence does **not** show standard naloxone is ineffective — it shows repeat dosing and monitoring matter.
- **Rescue breathing and airway support matter at least as much as the antidote.** The cause of death and of permanent brain injury is lack of oxygen. Guidance emphasises rescue breaths especially where a sedative adulterant is involved.
- **Naloxone wears off before many opioids do.** Re-sedation after an apparently successful reversal is a documented pattern, which is why the person needs to be observed and transported, not left once awake.

**What naloxone does not do — the most important single fact in this note:**
- **It does not reverse benzodiazepines** or designer benzodiazepines ([[bromazolam]], [[clonazolam]]). There is no community antidote for these. (Flumazenil, a hospital drug, can precipitate withdrawal seizures and tachyarrhythmias and is not a safe default — [MMWR 72(52–53)](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/72/wr/mm725253a5.htm).)
- **It does not reverse alpha-2 agonists** — [[xylazine]], [[medetomidine]]. Public-health guidance is explicit: *although xylazine is not reversed by naloxone, administer naloxone due to the high likelihood that fentanyl or other opioids are present* ([Medi-Cal DUR bulletin](https://medi-calrx.dhcs.ca.gov/cms/medicalrx/static-assets/documents/provider/dur/educational-articles/dured_250807_Xylazine_in_Illicit_Drugs_Increases_Dangers_Associated_with_Overdose.pdf) · [Washington State guidance](https://covid19.wa.gov/sites/default/files/2024-03/150246-DCHSXylazineInvolvedOverdosePoster.pdf)). In the Chicago cluster **most confirmed medetomidine cases had no or only partial response to naloxone**, with bradycardia in 87%.
- **It does not reverse alcohol, ketamine, GHB, or stimulants.**
- Consequence: **someone who stays sedated after naloxone has not "failed" the naloxone** — they are sedated by something else, and still need emergency care and airway support. Public guidance notes that the goal is restored breathing, not restored consciousness, and that continuing to give naloxone to someone who is breathing normally but groggy is not the aim; excess naloxone carries its own problems ([STAT](https://www.statnews.com/2023/06/21/xylazine-tranq-opioid-overdose-naloxone/)).
- **It does not reverse [[btmps]]** or other non-psychoactive adulterants.

## 2. Alpha-2 agonist intoxication and withdrawal (medetomidine, xylazine)

**Intoxication — recognise:** deep prolonged sedation out of proportion to the naloxone response · **bradycardia** (heart rates as low as 32 bpm reported) · hypertension · pinpoint pupils · altered mental status. In the Chicago cluster: hypertension 95%, bradycardia 87%, altered mental status 84%, pinpoint pupils 84%.

**Withdrawal — recognise, and take seriously:** this is the syndrome most likely to be mistaken for something minor. Per CDC HAN: **tachycardia >100 bpm, severe hypertension, fluctuating alertness, tremor, chest pain, intractable nausea and vomiting**, beginning **within hours of last use and peaking 18–36 hours later** ([CDC HAN 00527](https://www.cdc.gov/han/php/notices/han00527.html)).

**Why it is an emergency:** in the Philadelphia series of **165 hospitalised patients**, the syndrome was life-threatening, required substantial escalation of care beyond typical opioid or xylazine withdrawal, produced **PRES and non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction**, was resistant to the medications previously effective for fentanyl and xylazine withdrawal, and **responded to dexmedetomidine — a hospital-only drug** ([MMWR 74(15):266–268](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/74/wr/mm7415a2.htm); the CDC erratum is **74(17):296, 15 May 2025** and corrects **reference 1 only — no figure changes** ([erratum](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/74/wr/mm7417a3.htm))). **150/165 (91%) required ICU care and 39/165 (24%) were intubated — verified 2026-11-09.** A Pittsburgh series of 23 patients found the same ([MMWR 74(15):269–271](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/74/wr/mm7415a3.htm)).

**The public-health message:** there is **no community treatment for this**. Severe vomiting, chest pain, a pounding racing heart or a severe headache during withdrawal from an illicit opioid supply means **go to hospital or call emergency services**. It is not something to wait out. Fentanyl and benzodiazepine test strips do not detect medetomidine, and a negative strip does not rule it out ([[drug-checking]]).

## 3. Serotonin syndrome

**Context:** arises from serotonergic excess, typically from combining agents — and the grey market produces this by accident, since an unregulated product can contain an undeclared serotonergic compound (`risk:serotonergic-interaction`). Onset is typically **hours to a day** after a new agent or dose change, which distinguishes it from neuroleptic malignant syndrome (days to weeks).

**Recognise the triad:**
- **Neuromuscular:** this is the discriminating feature — **hyperreflexia, clonus (especially inducible ankle clonus), tremor, myoclonus, rigidity**. Serotonin syndrome patients are **hyper**reflexic; NMS presents with hypo­reflexia and severe lead-pipe rigidity.
- **Autonomic:** tachycardia, hypertension or labile blood pressure, diaphoresis, mydriasis, diarrhoea, **hyperthermia**.
- **Mental state:** agitation, confusion, anxiety, delirium.

**Two important nuances from the literature:**
- **Fever is not required.** A 2020 review found serotonin syndrome can occur **in the absence of an elevated temperature** ([J Integr Neurosci 2020](https://article.imrpress.com/journal/JIN/19/4/10.31083/j.jin.2020.04.314/JIN2020314.pdf)), so a normal temperature does not exclude it.
- The **Hunter criteria** are the most commonly used decision rule and are widely described as the most sensitive, but this is **contested**: the same 2020 review concluded Hunter *could not* be regarded as a gold standard because it missed more cases than two other criteria sets. Emergency-medicine reviews stress that **recognition** is the core skill and that high suspicion is warranted with non-specific symptoms ([Mayo EM blog](https://emblog.mayo.edu/?p=1086)).
- For the exact criteria, the primary sources are Dunkley et al. 2003 (*QJM*) and Werneke et al. 2016 (*BMC Neurology*) — **not retrieved in full in this research**, so the criteria are not reproduced here.

**Response:** call emergency services. Severe cases require intensive care. Management is stopping the causative agents and supportive care in a monitored setting — **not** something to attempt outside hospital, and specifically **not** a reason to take anything further.

## 4. Hyperthermia

**Recognise:** hot, often (but not always) dry or sweaty skin · core temperature rising · agitation or confusion progressing to collapse · muscle rigidity · seizures · **dark, tea- or cola-coloured urine and falling urine output** (rhabdomyolysis — see [[health-risks/organ-specific]]).

**Why it is a true emergency and not "overheating":** sustained core hyperthermia causes protein denaturation, disseminated intravascular coagulation, multi-organ failure and death, and the time window is short. Hyperthermia was a prominent feature of the bromazolam cluster alongside seizures and myocardial injury ([MMWR 72(52–53)](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/72/wr/mm725253a5.htm)), and is a recognised feature of synthetic cathinone toxicity ([Groenewegen et al. 2024, PMID 38411851](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38411851/)). Hyperthermia, agitation and rhabdomyolysis also form the pathway to acute kidney injury.

**Response:** call emergency services immediately. Move to a cool environment while waiting. Definitive cooling and the management of rhabdomyolysis, hyperkalaemia and renal failure are hospital interventions.

## 5. Anaphylaxis

**Context in this area:** the TGA's advisory on unapproved peptides lists **severe allergic reactions and generalised hypersensitivity** among reports received ([TGA](https://www.tga.gov.au/node/575382)). Injected peptides, peptide impurities and aggregates are credible immunogens ([[health-risks/injection-and-contamination]]).

**Recognise — the life-threatening features are airway, breathing and circulation:**
- **Airway:** swelling of tongue, lips, throat; hoarse voice; sensation of throat closing; stridor.
- **Breathing:** wheeze, chest tightness, difficulty breathing, cyanosis.
- **Circulation:** feeling faint, collapse, pallor and clamminess, rapid weak pulse, loss of consciousness.
- Skin changes (urticaria, flushing, angioedema) are common and useful but **may be absent**, and skin changes alone are not anaphylaxis.
- Onset is usually within minutes of injection but can be delayed.

**Published guidance (Resuscitation Council UK framework):** recognition that the person is seriously unwell plus an **early call for help**, with assessment and treatment on an **ABCDE** approach; the 2021 RCUK update emphasises **intramuscular adrenaline as first-line treatment** in initial management. Positioning guidance from RCUK-derived material: lie the person flat with legs raised if breathing is not impaired, sitting may ease breathing if it is, and left lateral position in pregnancy. Anyone who has used an adrenaline auto-injector should still call emergency services. ([RCUK materials](https://www.resus.org.uk/sites/default/files/2020-06/EmergencyTreatmentOfAnaphylacticReactionsPPT.pdf) · [Allergy UK HCP resources](https://www.allergyuk.org/hcp-resources/recognising-managing-and-treating-severe-allergic-reactions/) · [NHS Scotland pathway](https://rightdecisions.scot.nhs.uk/media/icni4wab/anaphylaxis-recognition-and-management-v4-482.pdf))
- **No doses are given here.** Adult adrenaline dosing quoted on commercial training sites was not verifiable against a primary guideline in this research and is deliberately omitted. Consult resus.org.uk or local guidance.
- **Biphasic reactions occur**, which is why observation in hospital is part of standard guidance even after apparent recovery.

## 6. Pyrogenic (endotoxin) reaction

**Recognise:** **30 minutes to ~6 hours after an injection** — shaking chills/rigors, rapid-onset fever, severe myalgia, headache, nausea, tachycardia, and in severe cases hypotension. See [[health-risks/injection-and-contamination]] for the mechanism and the finding that ~15% of sampled grey-market peptides carried measurable endotoxin ([Apr 2026 preprint](https://www.preprints.org/manuscript/202604.1748), preprint-grade).

**Why this one needs emergency assessment rather than waiting:** a pyrogenic reaction is **clinically indistinguishable at the bedside** from early **sepsis**, from an injection-site infection becoming systemic, and from the SIRS presentations the TGA has received reports of. Sepsis is time-critical and endotoxaemia itself can produce hypotension and organ dysfunction. "It was probably just a bad batch" is a retrospective diagnosis, and a person cannot make it prospectively about themselves.

**Response:** call emergency services or attend urgent care, especially with fever plus any of confusion, breathlessness, a fast weak pulse, not passing urine, mottled skin, or rigors. **Tell clinicians an injection was given, when, and that the product was unregulated with uncertain identity and sterility.** Also seek assessment for expanding redness, swelling, pain or a firm/fluctuant area at an injection site — abscess and necrotising infection need surgical review, not time.

## 7. Hypoglycaemia

**Context:** relevant wherever insulin, a secretagogue, or an incretin agonist is involved — and particularly where a product's **identity or concentration is wrong**. The documented precedents are real: the 2024 FDA alert on compounded semaglutide found patients drawing **5–20× the intended dose** ([FDA alert via Healio](https://www.healio.com/news/endocrinology/20240909/fda-warns-of-adverse-events-due-to-overdosing-of-compounded-semaglutide)), and the TGA documented a product **sold as retatrutide that contained undeclared semaglutide and no retatrutide** ([TGA](https://www.tga.gov.au/node/575382)).

**Recognise:**
- **Early/autonomic:** sweating, tremor, palpitations, hunger, anxiety, pallor.
- **Neuroglycopenic:** confusion, slurred speech, odd or aggressive behaviour, incoordination, drowsiness — **this stage is frequently mistaken for intoxication**, which is how it gets missed in exactly this population.
- **Severe:** seizure, unconsciousness.
- Warning signs can be **blunted or absent** in people with long-standing diabetes or recurrent hypoglycaemia.

**Response:** this is a medical emergency when the person is confused, seizing or unconscious — **call emergency services**. Do not try to give anything by mouth to someone who is not fully alert and able to swallow safely. Per the FDA alert, **a prolonged period of observation and treatment may be necessary because of semaglutide's ~1-week half-life** — i.e. the hypoglycaemia risk from an incretin overdose is not a short event, and this is a reason the person needs to be in a monitored setting rather than at home.

## Cross-cutting

- **Clinicians cannot test for most of these.** Routine urine immunoassays do not detect nitazenes, designer benzodiazepines, most cathinones, medetomidine, or any peptide. Most of the case literature cited across this area relies on self-reported exposure. **What the person says was taken is often the only diagnostic information available.**
- **Several of these syndromes look alike early** and cannot be distinguished in the field: hyperthermia vs serotonin syndrome vs NMS; pyrogenic reaction vs sepsis; hypoglycaemia vs intoxication; alpha-2 withdrawal vs stimulant toxicity. Field triage is not the place to decide.
- **Withdrawal can be the emergency.** Both severe designer-benzodiazepine withdrawal (seizures, status epilepticus) and medetomidine withdrawal (hypertensive emergency, PRES, myocardial infarction) are life-threatening *because* the drug stopped.
- **One person remaining able to call is the intervention that makes all of the above possible.**
