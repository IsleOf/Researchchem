# The pharmaceutical stimulant market: shortage, counterfeits, telehealth

**One-line summary:** A prescription stimulant shortage that began in October 2022 is still not formally resolved in late 2026; the resulting substitution into informal supply meets a counterfeit pill market in which "Adderall" tablets have repeatedly been found to contain **methamphetamine**, and the regulatory response has split between a criminal prosecution of a telehealth prescriber and a fifth consecutive temporary extension of telehealth prescribing flexibilities.

## 1. The shortage, 2022–2026

- FDA listed **amphetamine mixed salts** in shortage in **October 2022**, originally triggered by a manufacturing delay at one producer and then sustained by demand growth and DEA production quotas.
- **As of mid-October 2026, generic immediate-release mixed amphetamine salts tablets were still listed in active shortage** on FDA's drug shortage database, with a reported 19 of 73 listings unavailable. Extended-release mixed amphetamine salts capsules were reported *not* on the current list, though sources disagree by strength. **These figures come from patient-facing secondary summaries** ([example](https://www.takeroon.com/blog/adderall-shortage-2026), [example](https://www.medfinder.com/blog/adderall-ir-shortage-update-what-patients-need-to-know-in-2026)) — **verify against the FDA Drug Shortages database before citing.**
- DEA **raised amphetamine production quotas in October 2025 and again for 2026** (one source puts the dextroamphetamine increase at ~25%). Supply has not equilibrated and **FDA has announced no resolution date**. Methylphenidate availability has been more intermittent than persistently short.
- Practical consequence: four years of discontinuous treatment for a patient group whose condition is treated with a Schedule II drug that cannot legally be stockpiled, refilled early, or easily transferred between pharmacies. This is the demand-side driver for everything in sections 2 and 3.

## 2. Counterfeit "Adderall" and methylphenidate pills: what analysis finds

**What is consistently found:** methamphetamine, in tablets visually indistinguishable from genuine product.

- **DEA laboratory analysis** of pills seized in Louisville found tablets "made to look exactly like Adderall" that **contained methamphetamine** ([DEA 2019 warning](https://www.dea.gov/press-releases/2019/08/05/dea-issues-warning-over-counterfeit-pills)).
- A UCLA-led ethnographic and **drug-checking study of tourist-oriented pharmacies in northern Mexico** tested 45 pill samples; **nine samples sold as Adderall contained methamphetamine** ([medRxiv preprint](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2023.01.27.23285123.full.pdf); [UCLA Health release](https://www.uclahealth.org/news/release/counterfeit-pills-sold-mexican-pharmacies-found-contain-fentanyl-heroin-and-methamphetamine)). The authors' central finding for harm reduction: **counterfeits cannot be identified by appearance.**
- A **New Mexico harm-reduction adulterant-checking snapshot (March 2025)** reported Adderall pills containing methamphetamine among 32 tested samples ([PDF](https://www.nmharmreduction.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/March-2025-ACP-Snapshot.pdf)) — small sample, local programme data.
- Scale: the **US State Department's 2025 report to Congress on counterfeit prescription medication** describes counterfeit pills sold by unregistered Mexican pharmacies laced with fentanyl, methamphetamine and other potent drugs, cites CDC data suggesting **~4.7% of US overdose deaths involve counterfeit prescription pills**, and reports **DEA seizing more than 47 million counterfeit pills in 2025** ([PDF](https://www.state.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/Report-Counterfeit-Prescription-Medication-Report-006134-Accessible-07.03.2025.pdf)).
- Where counterfeit tablets contain fentanyl, DEA's own lethality statistics are the relevant anchor: **2 in 5** fentanyl-containing counterfeit pills carried a potentially lethal dose in 2021, rising to **6 in 10** in 2022 ([DEA 2021 public safety alert](https://www.dea.gov/alert/sharp-increase-fake-prescription-pills-containing-fentanyl-and-meth)). A 2026 seizure reported in the press involved **74 000 counterfeit Adderall pills containing methamphetamine** alongside >1 000 fentanyl-containing counterfeit oxycodone pills.

**What is notably absent:** no peer-reviewed forensic characterisation of **counterfeit methylphenidate** tablets was found, and no 2025–26 peer-reviewed quantitative analysis of counterfeit stimulant tablet *content and dose variability* was found. The evidence base is DEA press material, government reports, and a small number of drug-checking studies. **This is a real gap, not an absence of the phenomenon** — see [[stimulants/open-questions]].

**Three distinct substitution risks for someone who cannot fill a prescription:**
1. A counterfeit tablet containing **methamphetamine** at an unknown dose — different kinetics, different cardiovascular load, different dependence liability from prescribed amphetamine.
2. A counterfeit tablet containing **fentanyl** — the single highest-lethality outcome, and the reason stimulant-seeking now carries opioid overdose risk. Naloxone relevance applies to anyone using non-pharmacy pills of any description.
3. A **cathinone NPS** sold as a stimulant alternative — see [[stimulants/cathinone-churn]].

## 3. Telehealth prescribing: prosecution and perpetual extension

Two opposing regulatory signals run in parallel.

**The prosecution.** Done Global, a subscription ADHD telehealth company, was indicted in the **Northern District of California in June 2024** over an alleged scheme to distribute Adderall and other stimulants; DOJ alleged **>40 million tablets** prescribed illegitimately ([DOJ press release](https://www.justice.gov/usao-ndca/pr/digital-health-company-and-medical-practice-indicted-100m-adderall-distribution-scheme)). Founder **Ruthia He** and clinical president **David Brody** were **convicted by a San Francisco jury in late 2025**; a **December 2025 superseding indictment** added the company and a physician-owned professional corporation, alleging continued violations through February 2025. Reported sentences: **He six years and a $1 million fine; Brody two years and a separate $1 million fine**. Law-firm analysis describes it as **DOJ's first criminal drug-distribution prosecution arising from telemedicine prescribing practices** ([Ropes & Gray alert](https://www.ropesgray.com/en/insights/alerts/2026/01/dojs-done-global-telehealth-prosecution-signals-expanded-criminal-risk); [ArentFox Schiff on sentencing](https://www.afslaw.com/perspectives/investigations-blog/done-global-founder-and-clinical-president-sentenced-90-million)). The alleged mechanism was structural rather than individual: subscription model, advertising-driven intake, and pay structures and clinical workflows that rewarded prescribing volume. The defendants disputed the charges. **Sentencing details are from news and law-firm reporting; verify against DOJ announcements.**

**The extension.** Simultaneously, **DEA and HHS issued a fourth temporary extension of COVID-era telemedicine controlled-substance flexibilities, covering 1 January – 31 December 2026**, allowing Schedule II–V prescribing (including amphetamines) by audio-video telemedicine **without a prior in-person examination**. The **special registration rule proposed in January 2025 had still not been finalised as of late October 2026** ([Healthcare Dive](https://www.healthcaredive.com/news/dea-hhs-extend-telehealth-controlled-substance-prescriptions-flexibilities-fourth-time/808735/)).

**The resulting controversy, fairly stated.** The genuine tension is that the same flexibility that makes stimulant treatment reachable for rural, disabled and underserved patients during a four-year shortage is also the mechanism the Done prosecution targeted. Neither "telehealth ADHD prescribing is a pill mill" nor "it is simply access" is supported as a general claim: the prosecuted conduct involved specific alleged workflow and incentive design, and **no study was found quantifying diversion or inappropriate-prescribing rates across telehealth stimulant prescribing as a whole**. Flagged in [[stimulants/open-questions]].

## Harm-reduction implications

- A shortage gap is a **substitution event**, and the substitutes available are riskier than the medicine, not equivalent to it.
- **Appearance, imprint and packaging do not authenticate a tablet.** This is the one finding that every forensic and drug-checking source above agrees on.
- Pills obtained outside a pharmacy should be treated as potentially fentanyl-containing regardless of what they are sold as.
- Continuity-of-care measures (prescriber contact before running out, pharmacy-level supply checks, documented regimen changes) are the harm-reduction intervention with the best evidence base here, which is to say: the boring one.
