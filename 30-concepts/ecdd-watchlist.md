# ECDD watchlist: which circulating substances are likely to get international review next

**Concept:** [[polydrug-risk]] · **Related:** [[depressants/index]] · [[stimulants/index]] · [[market-structure/global-regional]] · [[market-structure/scheduling-churn]]

**Scope.** This is a regulatory-forecasting note. It predicts which substances **already circulating and already detected** in this repo are most likely to receive WHO Expert Committee on Drug Dependence (ECDD) attention next. It does not propose, design or evaluate new molecules, and it says nothing about relative safety. See Section 5.

## Section 1: the current review slate

The eight substances below were named in the US Federal Register notice of 26 Aug 2026 requesting comments ahead of WHO ECDD review ([Federal Register notice](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/08/26/2026-17380/international-drug-scheduling-single-convention-on-narcotic-drugs-convention-on-psychotropic)).

Two repo notes also cite a Federal Register document number, 2026-17380, for a different DEA notice (see Section 5).

|---|---|---|
| Designer benzodiazepines | clobromazolam (phenazolam); desalkylgidazepam (bromonordiazepam); ethylbromazolam | [[depressants/designer-benzodiazepines]]: phenazolam and ethylbromazolam are both described as rising in 2026 detections |
| Orphines (benzimidazol-2-ones) | cychlorphine (N-propionitrile chlorphine); spirochlorphine (R6980) | [[depressants/nitazenes]] (orphine section) |

**What the grouping reveals about current regulator priorities.**
- **Designer benzodiazepines are the largest group (three of eight).** Regulators are now reacting to a benzodiazepine family whose members are sold as counterfeit alprazolam, and the repo's own timeline shows that drug-checking services detected successors (ethylbromazolam, Nov 2024) well before regulators did.
- **Opioids are the second priority (three of eight, across two classes).** The orphine and nitazene entries show the opioid focus that the repo's depressants notes describe.
- **Two entries are licensed medicines or veterinary drugs repurposed into the illicit supply (etomidate, medetomidine).** This is a different kind of priority: the regulatory question is about familiar drugs in new formats and routes, not clandestine chemistry. The repo's etomidate note argues that early-warning systems are poorly designed for this case.
- **No stimulant appears on the slate.** Consistent with the stimulants watchlist, regulatory and analytical attention in 2026 is concentrated on sedatives and opioids, so a stimulant signal would need to be larger before it reached this stage.

## Section 2: the method, as published

**Source status.** The full current ECDD methodology document was **not** retrieved. This section is a reconstruction from WHO summary pages (https://www.who.int/groups/ecdd/about-us/how-we-work and https://ecddrepository.org/en/frequently_asked_questions), as summarised in the brief. Check the primary methodology document before relying on any detail here.

**Two stages.**
1. **Pre-review:** is there enough robust scientific information to proceed to a full assessment?
2. **Critical review:** full assessment of the substance.

**Triggers for a critical review.** A critical review can be triggered by:
- a treaty Party notifying WHO that it is scheduling a substance;
- the Commission on Narcotic Drugs (CND) explicitly requesting a review;
- a pre-review recommending one;
- WHO learning that a substance is clandestinely manufactured and poses a serious risk, with no recognised therapeutic use.

**Who can start a pre-review.** The ECDD Secretary, a Committee member, or an observer. A pre-review is normally carried out if the substance is used as a medicine.

**Evaluation criteria.** Evidence of dependence potential; current or likely public health and social problems associated with use; therapeutic applications.

**Prioritisation.** The Secretariat runs a substance prioritisation process that feeds the working group.

**Repo-recorded schedule, unverified here.** The source registry in this repo records a 49th ECDD meeting on 19–22 Oct 2026, with critical reviews listed for cychlorphine, desalkylgidazepam, ethylbromazolam, etomethazene and spirochlorphine, and preliminary reviews for etomidate and medetomidine. This was not confirmed against a WHO page in this session. If it is accurate, the slate in Section 1 is already in the review process, and the forecasting question shifts from "will it be reviewed" to "what comes next".

## Section 3: derived criteria

These are observable signals that predict review, derived from Section 2.

| Signal | Why it predicts review | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| **Already nationally scheduled by a Party** | This is itself a listed trigger (a Party notifying WHO about scheduling). | **Strongest single predictor** |
| Multi-jurisdiction detection | Indicates spread beyond one national market, and feeds the prioritisation process. | Strong |
| Deaths attributed (even as contributory) | Public health problem criterion. | Strong, but attribution is often weak |
| No therapeutic use | Makes the "no recognised therapeutic use" trigger applicable, and removes a medicine-route pre-review. | Moderate |
| Clandestine manufacture with serious risk | Matches the fourth trigger directly. | Moderate; hard to observe from the outside |
| Use as a licensed medicine | Normally leads to pre-review. This pushes the other way from the "no therapeutic use" trigger. | Moderate; route-dependent |
| Absence from routine toxicology | Raises the chance that deaths go unrecorded, so it weakens the public health criterion's evidence base. It is a reason for concern rather than a trigger. | Weak as a predictor |
| Already internationally controlled | The substance has passed the review stage, so it is no longer a candidate for new review. | Excludes |

**Why national scheduling is the strongest predictor.** The first listed trigger is a Party notifying WHO about a scheduling decision. A national scheduling decision is therefore the most direct observable step toward a critical review, which is why it outweighs the other signals in the scoring below. Note that a national decision is not automatically a WHO notification. It makes one likely, but the timing is not observable from national notices alone.

**Count.** 24 substances scored. This count does not include the eight slate substances in Section 1, which are already named.

**Top five predictions (highest predicted review likelihood).**
1. **2-MMC (high).** It is already controlled in the EU and the Netherlands, which is the strongest predictor, and it is the active substitution trigger for the next wave.
2. **Bromazolam (high).** It is already emergency-scheduled in the US and detected in many jurisdictions with overdose cases; its absence from the Aug 2026 slate is the main open question.
3. **7-OH (high, conditional).** WHO has already pre-reviewed kratom and 7-OH with contributory-death notes; a US federal order is the trigger to watch.
4. **N-isopropylbutylone (medium).** It is the clearest US replacement event, and it fits the successor pattern, but national scheduling is not documented.
5. **Xylazine (medium).** It has a large death count and a veterinary medicine status, but it is being displaced regionally by medetomidine, which is already on the slate.

## Section 5: limits

- **This forecasts regulatory attention on substances already in circulation.** It does not predict, propose or design novel structures, and it must not be used to do so.
- **Scheduling forecasts say nothing about relative safety.** A substance being reviewed, or not reviewed, is not evidence about how harmful it is. Several high-scoring entries (for example 2-MMC) have weak death data, and several low-scoring entries (for example eutylone) have large historical death counts.
- **Scores depend on repo data that is partly secondary or unverified.** Key unverified items include the flubromazolam US scheduling, the 7-OH poison-centre figures, the xylazine international status, and the Federal Register text for the 26 Aug 2026 notice.
- **Document-number discrepancy.** The kratom note cites Federal Register document 2026-17380 (26 Aug 2026) for the DEA temporary scheduling of mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, MGM-15 and MGM-16. The brief cites the same number for the ECDD notice. A single document number cannot refer to both. One of the two citations is wrong or the notice numbering is ambiguous. Resolve against the Federal Register before citing either.
- **Analytical visibility bias.** Detection-based scoring favours substances that laboratories test for. The repo notes that nitazenes, orphines, medetomidine and BTMPS are missed by routine toxicology, so some high-harm substances may be under-scored.
- **Method reconstruction.** Section 2 is not the primary WHO methodology. Scoring rules in Section 3 are derived from it and may change once the primary document is read.
- **No dosing, synthesis, precursor, vendor or sourcing information** is included, by design.
